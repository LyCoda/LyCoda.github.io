Taking a research robot towards a CE mark

A regulatory assessment written as the researcher commercialising a published lung-ultrasound scanning robot in the EU: qualification, risk class, quality system, documentation and standards.

My roleIndividual coursework
ContextUCL · MPHY0046 Medical Robotics and Devices
Project stageMSc coursework · 2026

TL;DR

The question

What risk class would you give this robot?

What I found

Class IIa, under Annex VIII Rule 10, because it is an active device used for diagnosis.

What I did

I argued the robot should be a Class IIa active diagnostic device under Annex VIII Rule 10, with a deliberately narrow intended purpose: clinician-supervised image acquisition, not automated diagnosis. I then mapped the quality system, technical file and standards a notified body would expect to see.

Tools & methods

EU MDR 2017/745, Annex VIII classification rules, EN ISO 13485, ISO 14971, IEC 62304, IEC 62366-1, IEC 60601 series

The device

The system, published by Tan et al. (2023), is a research-stage robot that scans both sides of the chest with two ultrasound probes. It combines a gantry-style dual-arm platform, structured-light reconstruction of the chest surface, automatic probe-path planning, closed-loop force control and a clinician interface.

The paper reports scans in under four minutes per person, with image quality comparable to manual scanning. Those are the authors' results; my work was to set out what it would take to place the device on the EU market.

Defining the claim before the device

Under the MDR, the manufacturer's intended purpose decides whether a product is a medical device and how strictly it is regulated. I defined it conservatively: an active, non-invasive, clinician-supervised imaging device that acquires lung ultrasound images to support bedside assessment of respiratory disease.

It is still a medical device, because acquiring images is part of the diagnostic chain even when a clinician interprets them, and its action is mechanical and acoustic rather than pharmacological. Claiming automated diagnosis would have pulled in a heavier evidence burden the research does not support.

Choosing the classification rule

Classification came from testing each candidate rule against the device, not from picking the most familiar one.

EU MDR Annex VIII rules considered
RuleApplies?Reason
Rules 1–4Not decisiveRule 1 is the default for non-invasive devices, but the more specific Rule 10 for active diagnostic devices takes precedence
Rules 5–8NoThey cover invasive devices; the probes touch intact skin only
Rule 9NoIt covers active therapeutic devices; nothing is treated
Rule 10Yes, Class IIaAn active device intended for diagnosis and monitoring
Rule 11NoThe software drives the hardware rather than standing alone, so it takes the device's class

Quality system and technical file

A Class IIa device under Annex IX needs a quality management system; EN ISO 13485:2016 is the accepted framework. For a system that combines robotics, imaging, software and patient contact, it also keeps every subsystem under the same design controls, change control and supplier checks.

  1. ConceptIntended purpose, users, patient population and system boundary.
  2. DesignArchitecture, drawings, schematics and a risk file covering probe force, positioning errors, electrical safety, EMC, software faults and poor images.
  3. VerificationSoftware tests, bench tests of motion and force control, electrical safety, EMC, usability and image-quality validation.
  4. ClinicalClinical evaluation and, if needed, an investigation with protocol and adverse-event procedures.
  5. MarketSupplier control, traceability, complaints, corrective actions, vigilance and the post-market surveillance plan.

The supporting standards were ISO 14971 for risk management, IEC 62304 for the software lifecycle, IEC 62366-1 for usability and IEC 60601-1, 60601-1-2 and 60601-2-37 for electrical safety, EMC and ultrasound equipment. Inside the company, the person responsible for regulatory compliance keeps the file current; outside it, a notified body audits it and issues the certificate behind the CE mark.

Why this matters for an engineer

A working prototype and a device that can legally be sold are different things. This assessment made that concrete: most of the work lies in evidence, traceability and controlled change, not in the robot itself.

It connects my clinical follow-up work at KOLN 3D and the adoption plan in OsteoTrack. The classification here is my reasoned coursework argument, not a regulatory determination.
