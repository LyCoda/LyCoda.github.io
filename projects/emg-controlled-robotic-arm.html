Turning muscle activity into a working grasp

An Arduino-controlled, tendon-driven hand and wrist, bringing surface EMG sensing, analogue electronics and 3D-printed mechanics into one prototype.

The design brief

My undergraduate dissertation investigated a low-cost EMG-controlled prosthetic-arm concept using desktop 3D printing and Arduino-compatible electronics. The target was basic grasping within a parts budget below £100, excluding in-house printing labour.

The final scope was a hand and wrist, without an articulated elbow. It was an engineering proof of concept, rather than a patient-ready prosthesis. I worked across sensing, electronics, firmware, CAD, assembly and initial evaluation under the supervision of Dr James May.

From a muscle signal to a command

Surface electrodes captured muscle activity. Because the small, rapidly changing signal could not directly drive a motor, the analogue front end amplified it, rectified it and filtered it into an envelope related to contraction intensity. A DC offset made that signal compatible with the Arduino's input range.

The design used an AD8226 instrumentation amplifier and TL084 stages for rectification and filtering. The important system-level choice was to condition the biological signal before applying a deliberately simple control rule.

  1. SenseSurface electrodes detect muscle activity.
  2. ConditionAmplification, rectification and filtering produce an envelope.
  3. InterpretArduino estimates activity relative to a changing baseline.
  4. ActuateServo pulleys pull the tendons to close the hand.

The firmware used a running root-mean-square estimate and an adaptive baseline. Separate thresholds for closing and opening introduced hysteresis, reducing repeated switching near a single threshold. A short post-command interval gave the servos time to respond.

This was binary open/close control, not independent finger classification or proportional grip control. That limit kept the algorithm understandable and the hardware cost manageable.

Mechanical iteration

The hand used PLA finger segments and a tendon-and-pulley transmission driven by three hobby servos. Fishing line provided a low-cost tendon material, while the forearm housed the motors, electronics and wiring.

Design changes made to simplify assembly and improve consistency
Part Problem Design response
Fingers Segments could skew under tendon tension Reinforced hinge geometry, pins and centred tendon channels
Palm Wall flex and friction around tendon exits Thicker local geometry and an accessible palm lid
Wrist A geared concept introduced backlash and print-tolerance demands A simpler ball-and-socket direction with tendon anchors
Forearm Fragile segmented clips and trapped wiring A shell with removable access panels and internal mounting features

Repair access mattered as much as initial assembly. The palm lid exposed tendon adjustment points; removable forearm panels made the servos and electronics accessible. Standardising finger geometry also reduced the number of unique replacement parts.

The trade-off was coupled movement. Sharing actuators and tendon routes lowered cost and complexity, while limiting fine dexterity and making slack and friction central performance constraints.

Keeping sensing and actuation stable

Servo current demand and analogue signal quality had to be considered together. The architecture separated the EMG supply from the servo power rails, with a common grounding arrangement and attention to routing noisy power wiring away from the sensing path.

The integration work involved electrode placement, baseline calibration, mechanical tendon tension and power stability. A threshold that worked well in one setup could become unreliable when contact shifted or the mechanical load changed.

Those interactions were the main lesson of the build: a clean control diagram does not remove the need to test the complete physical system.

Initial evaluation

The presentation summary reports approximately £91 in parts and around 6 N of combined fingertip force from simple scale measurements. These are prototype-level observations, not independent product benchmarks.

Reported fingertip measurements from the presentation summary
Digit Flexion Fingertip force
Index 61.2° 1.42 N
Middle 59.8° 1.50 N
Ring 48.5° 0.98 N
Little finger 44.3° 0.97 N
Thumb 46.5° 1.32 N

A short timing test reported five values between 264 and 288 ms, averaging approximately 275 ms. The summary describes the measurement endpoints inconsistently, so this should be read as an indicative control-timing observation rather than a verified muscle-to-motion latency.

The control test was also too small for a dependable accuracy claim. Its stated percentage does not reconcile with the listed event counts. A larger, consistently labelled trial is needed before reporting control accuracy as a performance result.

These limitations do not erase the integration work; they define the next evaluation task. Mechanical force, complete movement time, missed commands and unintended commands need separate, repeatable protocols.

What I would improve next

My first priorities would be reducing tendon friction and slack, improving repeatability of electrode placement, and checking the power system under realistic loads. Durability and maintenance would need longer tests than the initial demonstration.

A better controller could use a shorter, carefully evaluated processing window and an additional EMG channel. Proportional grip or multiple gesture classes would then need to justify their added sensing, calibration and computational demands.

Before any clinical use, the design would require formal safety work, appropriate user studies, comfort and socket-fit evaluation, and robust long-term testing. The project established a platform for investigating those questions, not their answers.
