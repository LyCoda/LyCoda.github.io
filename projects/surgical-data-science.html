Three clinical datasets, three kinds of evidence

Three assignments on clinical data: scoring ultrasound image quality against experts, classifying surgical skill and COVID-19 CT scans, and clustering ECG beats without labels.

My roleIndividual coursework · three assignments
ContextUCL · MPHY0047 Surgical Data Science
Project stageMSc coursework · 2026

At a glance

Across all three I chose methods to fit the data: normality checks before choosing between Welch and Mann–Whitney tests, cross-validated classifiers judged on the error that matters clinically, and label-free clustering scored against the true classes only afterwards. Several results were negative, and I reported them as such.

Part 1 · Do image metrics agree with expert scores?

The dataset held transoesophageal echo images from 20 participants, 7 experts and 13 novices, across 10 views. Each image had two manual quality scores and a gold-standard reference. The two manual scores correlated positively in every view, most strongly in View 1.

I compared each image with its reference using structural similarity, mutual information and cosine similarity. Mutual information and cosine similarity differed significantly between experts and novices (p < 0.05, uncorrected across 30 tests) in 2 of 10 views; structural similarity did so in 1. A degree-7 polynomial ridge regression from these metrics to the manual scores reached R² ≈ 0.56 at best. Image misalignment, measured by ECC registration, showed no significant expert–novice difference in any view.

With about 20 images per view, those regression fits are in-sample and optimistic. I would now hold out data before reporting them.

Part 2 · Classifying surgical skill and COVID-19 CT

Using JIGSAWS robotic suturing data from four expert and four novice surgeons, I extracted 22 motion parameters. Completion time separated the groups most clearly: experts averaged 86.5 ± 14.5 s and novices 141.2 ± 52.9 s. Five features (time, path length and velocity for both instruments) fed three classifiers.

Cross-validated skill classification
ModelAccuracy · F1What stood out
Bagged decision tree0.800 · 0.809Baseline
Random forest0.825 · 0.834Fewest novices labelled as experts (4 of 20)
K-nearest neighbours0.825 · 0.855Missed only 1 expert trial, but labelled 6 novices as experts

I recommended the random forest: in surgical training, certifying a novice too early is the more dangerous error. The folds held out one trial per surgeon, so these scores do not test performance on unseen surgeons.

On 746 CT images, linear and quadratic discriminant analysis reached 0.71 and 0.58 accuracy, with QDA over-predicting COVID-19. HoG features with an RBF support vector machine reached 0.81. On an unbalanced subset (349 COVID-19, 197 non-COVID), the SVM reached 0.83 unweighted and 0.84 with class weighting, so most of the change came from the altered class mix. Published deep-learning models score higher, but they use far richer training, so the comparison is not like-for-like.

Part 3 · Clustering ECG beats without labels

1,800 heartbeats from three classes (normal, supraventricular ectopic and ventricular ectopic) were described by 83 features. PCA kept over 90% of the variance in far fewer dimensions. The clustering methods never saw the labels; the labels were used only to score them afterwards.

The weaker linkage methods collapsed almost every beat into one normal cluster. Supraventricular beats were the hardest class for every method that worked, with at best 455 of 600 grouped correctly (Ward linkage on PCA features), because they overlap with normal beats in feature space. The Gaussian mixture did best overall, probably because it lets clusters take different shapes and spreads.

What the three have in common

Each assignment came down to the same habits: check the assumptions before choosing a test, pick the metric that reflects the clinical cost of an error, and say plainly when a method fails. Those habits are also the main difference between this work and my second-year lab reports.
