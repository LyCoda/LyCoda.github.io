Building an ECG amplifier one stage at a time

A breadboard ECG amplifier, built up from a single op-amp to an instrumentation amplifier and band-pass filter, with every stage measured against its design value.

My roleDesigned, built and tested the circuit
ContextCity, University of London · EE2703 Biomedical Instrumentation
Project stageYear 2 lab project · 2024

At a glance

I built the ECG signal chain one block at a time and measured each one. Moving from a discrete differential amplifier to an INA121 instrumentation amplifier raised common-mode rejection from 32.6 dB to 59.0 dB, and the finished filter passed roughly 0.62–21 Hz against a 0.4–20 Hz design.

Tools & methods
TL084 and INA121 amplifiers, breadboard prototyping, Keysight oscilloscope, signal generator, multimeter

Why an ECG needs its own amplifier

The heart's electrical signal reaches the skin at only a few millivolts, and it shares the electrodes with mains interference, movement and breathing artefacts. A useful ECG front end has to amplify the small difference between two electrodes, reject what both electrodes see in common, and keep only the frequency band where the cardiac signal lives.

The lab series built that chain in order. Each stage was wired on a breadboard, powered from a split ±15 V supply, driven from a signal generator and judged on an oscilloscope, so the effect of every block could be seen before the next one was added.

  1. AmplifyInverting TL084 stage designed for a gain of 10.
  2. RejectDifferential amplifier designed for a gain of 18.
  3. RefineINA121 instrumentation amplifier set to a gain of 99 with one 510 Ω resistor.
  4. FilterSecond-order Butterworth high-pass (0.4 Hz) and low-pass (20 Hz) stages.
  5. TestA simulated cardiac waveform passed through the complete chain.

Design values against measurements

Every stage was compared with the value it was designed for. The gaps were small for single-chip stages and larger where matched resistor pairs mattered.

The instrumentation amplifier performed best largely because it needed fewer external parts. One gain resistor replaced several resistor pairs whose tolerances otherwise stack up and unbalance the inputs, which is exactly what limited the discrete differential amplifier's common-mode rejection.

Shaping the ECG band

The low-pass stage used 100 nF capacitors and 80 kΩ resistors with a gain of 1.57. It passed 1 Hz with a gain of 1.25, fell to 0.73 at 20 Hz against the 0.707 expected at the cut-off, and dropped to 0.17 at 1 kHz.

The high-pass stage used 680 nF capacitors and 581.5 kΩ resistors, chosen from the parts available. It attenuated 0.3 Hz as intended, but its gain above the cut-off recovered only to 0.87, lower than designed. Combined, the band-pass response peaked at about 8.9 dB between 5 and 10 Hz.

Where the noise came from

The report traced most of the remaining error to mismatched resistor values, component tolerance and the lab environment: mains-powered equipment, nearby devices and loose breadboard wiring. Battery power or isolation, a notch filter at the mains frequency and secure connections were the suggested remedies.

It also asked what would change to record muscle activity instead. EMG occupies a much wider band than ECG, so the filter cut-offs, interference filtering and electrodes would all need to change. A year later that question became the starting point of my EMG-controlled robotic arm.

Reading it back two years later

The circuit work holds up; the report shows how much my writing has changed since. It walks through the lab procedure step by step, takes a single reading at each setting and explains differences in words rather than with a tolerance or uncertainty estimate. Some voltage ratios are labelled as decibels.

Today I would repeat each measurement, predict the expected spread from component tolerances before testing, and separate method from findings. My later reports, such as the fNIRS dissertation and the surgical-workflow study, follow that structure.
